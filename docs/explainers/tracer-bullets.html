Build a thin path first, then thicken it

How Vela plans the work: one tracer bullet through every app, then slice after slice.

Most plans build one layer at a time: each team finishes its own app, and the apps meet at the end. Nothing works until everything works, and the surprises in the contracts between apps show up when they're most expensive to fix.

A tracer bullet does the opposite. The first thing anyone builds is the thinnest path that goes all the way through, end to end, across every app and contract it needs. It does very little, but it's real, and it runs. Everything after that thickens a path that already works.

Each thickening is a slice: one thin piece of the product built end to end, from what the user does to what gets stored, so it can ship and be tried on its own. The tracer is the first slice.

What it looks like for Heron

This is an illustration built from the Heron example, not the plan in its vault. Heron has three apps: the Desktop agent on each laptop, the Summarizer that writes the notes, and Consent and identity, which keeps who agreed to what.

The tracer is the thinnest version of one flow: a person who has switched Heron on gets a 7:30 digest written from their calendar alone. It's small, but it crosses all three apps and every contract between them. If the consent check or the note request is wrong, the tracer shows it in the first weeks, not in January.

How Vela carries it through

Vela carries the idea through every step of the journey, so the tracer the lead plans is the first thing every team builds, and the first ticket each team picks up.

Releases and epicsThe tracer is the first slice of release one: one flow, end to end, at its thinnest. Each slice after it adds something people can see.
DesignEach team's design names the smallest working path through its own app that the tracer needs (its walking skeleton).
BreakdownStory one is the team's piece of the tracer. Every later story is a slice someone can demo.
TicketsTracer tickets come first, so every team starts on the same thin path.

Vela flags a story nobody could demo on its own, like "set up the database", so it can be folded into the slice that needs it. It also warns when release one has no end-to-end flow, or when a team's first story doesn't cross all its parts.

Steered, not forced

Sometimes a piece just isn't ready. Heron's transcripts wait on an approval, so they can't be in the tracer, and forcing them in would stall everything. When that happens, the lead or the team says so. Vela records what was left out and why, and the rest of the plan carries on. Anyone reading the plan later can see the choice, and who made it.

Tracer bullets come from Andy Hunt and Dave Thomas's The Pragmatic Programmer (1999). The walking skeleton is Alistair Cockburn's name for the same idea inside one system.
